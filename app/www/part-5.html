Part 5: Books to Read Alongside

Read in this order. Each summary gives the key lessons, how it connects to this course and your code, and one thing to do after reading it.

Not investment advice. Reading makes you informed, not profitable. SEBI found 87.7% of individual F&O traders lost money in FY26; most of them had read some of these books too.

1. Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets: John J. Murphy

The textbook of technical analysis. Start here.

Key lessons: 1. Three premises: price discounts everything, prices move in trends, history tends to repeat (because human behaviour does). 2. Dow Theory: primary, secondary and minor trends; trends need confirmation (by volume and related indices); a trend is in force until clearly reversed. 3. Trend basics: higher highs/higher lows; support and resistance, and their role reversal; trendlines and channels; the importance of round numbers. 4. Volume (and open interest) confirm price: rising price on rising volume is healthy. 5. Patterns: reversal (head & shoulders, double tops/bottoms) vs continuation (triangles, flags), with measured targets. 6. Moving averages and oscillators: MAs for trending markets, oscillators (RSI, stochastics, MACD) for ranges; divergences as warnings. 7. Use multiple timeframes: weekly/monthly charts for direction, daily for timing. 8. Intermarket analysis: bonds, commodities, currencies and stocks influence each other (Part 1's crude/USD-INR/Fed links). 9. His "ten laws": map the trend, trade with it, find support/resistance, know how far to expect a pullback, draw trendlines, follow MAs, learn the turns, know the warning signs, trend is confirmed by volume.

Connects to: Part 2 entirely. chart.py, indicators.py, patterns.py. Do after: pick 5 Nifty stocks and label trend, S/R and one pattern on weekly and daily charts.

2. Technical Analysis Explained: Martin J. Pring

Deeper on trends, cycles and momentum.

Key lessons: 1. Weight of the evidence: never act on one indicator; look for several independent signals agreeing. 2. The market cycle model: markets move through primary trends tied to the business cycle. Pring's six "stages" describe how bonds turn first, then stocks, then commodities, as the economy moves from recession to recovery to overheating. 3. Momentum principles: momentum usually peaks before price. Divergences, overbought/oversold readings and momentum trendline breaks give early warnings. 4. Relative strength (stock vs index, sector vs market) to find leaders: the basis of momentum investing. 5. Pring's KST ("Know Sure Thing"): a smoothed, multi-timeframe momentum indicator combining several rates of change. 6. Sentiment and breadth: advance/decline lines, new highs/lows, and how crowds behave at extremes. 7. Long-term perspective: understanding where you are in the bigger cycle beats short-term signal hunting.

Connects to: Part 1 (macro → markets), Part 2 (momentum indicators), Part 3 (momentum factor). Do after: add a relative-strength line (stock ÷ Nifty) to chart.py. Which Nifty sectors are leading right now?

3. Technical Analysis of the Indian Stock Markets: Shriram Nerlekar

Applying the same tools to Indian stocks.

⚠️ I'm less certain of this book's exact contents than of the others. These are the lessons it's generally used for. Check them against the copy you read.

Key lessons: 1. The standard toolkit (trends, patterns, indicators) illustrated on Indian stocks and indices (Sensex/Nifty), so you see the methods on charts you recognise. 2. Indian market specifics that affect charts: trading sessions, circuit limits, corporate actions (bonus/split adjustments), liquidity differences between large and small caps. 3. Practical discipline: combine indicators, use stop-losses, avoid illiquid stocks where patterns are unreliable.

Connects to: Part 2 applied to NSE stocks. Do after: find two examples in the book, then pull the same stocks today with chart.py and see how those setups look now.

4. How to Make Money Trading with Charts: Ashwani Gujral

A practical trading book by an Indian trader, focused on setups and risk.

Key lessons: 1. Keep it simple: a few setups you know deeply beat dozens of indicators. 2. Trade with the trend: identify the trend first, then look for pullback entries (e.g. near a rising moving average or support) rather than chasing breakouts. 3. Breakouts need confirmation: volume and the broader market's direction. 4. The stop-loss is decided before entry, at a level that proves the trade wrong, and position size comes from the stop (exactly your --risk-pct). 5. Risk-reward: take trades only where the potential reward is a multiple of the risk. 6. Psychology: most losses come from breaking your own rules: averaging down, moving stops, overtrading after a loss. 7. Indian market context: applies all this to NSE stocks and index charts.

Connects to: Parts 2 & 4; engine.py stop/position sizing. Do after: write your own one-page trading plan: setup, entry, stop, target, max risk per trade (≤1%), max trades per week.

5. Options as a Strategic Investment: Lawrence G. McMillan

The options reference book. Read it to understand risk, not to start trading F&O.

Key lessons: 1. What options are: the right (not obligation) to buy/sell; intrinsic vs time value; why most options expire worthless and time decay works against buyers. 2. Pricing & the Greeks: the Black-Scholes model; delta, gamma, theta, vega; implied volatility (IV) is the key variable. Buying options when IV is high is expensive. 3. Strategies by objective: covered calls, protective puts, spreads (bull/bear, calendar), straddles/strangles, ratio spreads. Each has a defined payoff diagram and risk profile. 4. Selling options earns small, frequent profits but carries large tail risk (selling naked options can lose many times the premium in one move). 5. Volatility trading: strategies that bet on volatility rather than direction. 6. Follow-up action: plan in advance what you'll do if the stock moves against you. 7. Options as hedges: protective puts are insurance for a portfolio.

India-specific warning: most retail F&O activity is buying cheap weekly index options near expiry, which fights theta, high IV and professional sellers (Part 3B: Jane Street). SEBI's studies show ~9 in 10 individual F&O traders lose money. Connects to: Part 3B (who is on the other side); SEBI_RULES.md. Do after: paper-trade only. Draw payoff diagrams for a covered call and a protective put on a Nifty stock. No real F&O money until you've studied this book fully and can explain each Greek.

6. Quantitative Trading: Ernest P. Chan

How an individual can run a quant trading business. The book closest to your code.

Key lessons: 1. Retail can do quant, if they pick strategies big funds ignore (small capacity, niche markets) and stay disciplined. 2. Finding ideas: academic papers, blogs, books. Then judge them before backtesting: does it make economic sense? Is the Sharpe realistic? Can it survive costs? 3. Backtesting pitfalls: look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, data-snooping / overfitting, ignoring transaction costs, using bad data. (You met all of these in Parts 3–4.) 4. Sharpe ratio thinking: aim for strategies with a consistently positive, realistic Sharpe, and be suspicious of very high ones. 5. Mean reversion and pairs trading, with cointegration tests (your pairs.py). 6. Position sizing with the Kelly criterion: optimal leverage ≈ mean excess return ÷ variance. In practice use half-Kelly or less, because estimates are noisy. 7. Regime change: strategies stop working. Monitor live performance and have rules for turning a strategy off. 8. Execution systems: automate carefully, paper trade first, reconcile live results with the backtest.

Connects to: Parts 3 & 4; backtest.py, pairs.py, paper_trade.py, live_kite.py. Do after: add a Kelly-fraction calculator to metrics.py (mean daily excess return ÷ variance) and compare it with the position sizes you've been using.

7. The Psychology of Money: Morgan Housel

Why behaviour matters more than intelligence. The most important book on this list.

Key lessons: 1. No one's crazy: people's money decisions come from their own experiences (someone who lived through 2008 or COVID invests differently). 2. Luck and risk are siblings. Don't copy outcomes; judge decisions. 3. "Enough": the hardest skill is stopping the goalposts from moving. Never risk what you have and need for what you don't have and don't need. 4. Compounding: most of Warren Buffett's wealth came after age 65. Time in the market is the superpower. At 19, you have it. 5. Getting wealthy vs staying wealthy: staying wealthy requires survival: frugality, paranoia, avoiding ruin. 6. Tails drive everything: a few big winners produce most of the returns (in stocks, companies and careers). 7. Freedom: the highest dividend money pays is control over your time. 8. Wealth is what you don't see: it's money not spent. 9. Save, even without a specific goal: savings create room for error. 10. Reasonable > rational: a strategy you'll actually stick with beats a "perfect" one you'll abandon. 11. Volatility is the fee, not a fine: the price you pay for equity returns. 12. You're playing your own game: don't take cues from people with different time horizons (day traders vs long-term investors). 13. Leave room for error and expect surprises.

Connects to: everything. The checklist, the kill switch, "most F&O traders lose". Do after: write down your personal "enough" and your rules for what money you will never trade with.

8. One Up on Wall Street: Peter Lynch

Fundamental stock picking by one of the most successful fund managers ever (Fidelity Magellan, 1977–1990).

Key lessons: 1. Invest in what you know: everyday observations (products you use, shops that are always busy) can lead to ideas before Wall Street notices. That's only a starting point; then do the research. 2. Six stock categories, each with different expectations: - Slow growers (mature, dividends) - Stalwarts (large, steady ~10–12% growth, e.g. HUL, ITC types) - Fast growers (small, aggressive, where tenbaggers come from) - Cyclicals (autos, metals: timing matters; low P/E can mean a peak) - Turnarounds (recovering from trouble) - Asset plays (hidden value in land, cash or subsidiaries) 3. The two-minute drill: be able to explain why you own a stock, and what must happen for it to succeed, in two minutes. 4. Key numbers: earnings growth, P/E vs growth (PEG), debt, cash, inventories, dividends, insider buying, buybacks. 5. Perfect-stock traits: boring name or business, out of favour, spinoffs, niche, low institutional ownership, people need to keep buying it. 6. Avoid: the hottest stock in the hottest industry, "the next X", diversification into unrelated businesses ("diworsification"), and stocks driven only by whispers. 7. Don't try to time the market or predict the economy. Focus on companies; stay invested; expect declines. 8. Tenbaggers (10× stocks) need time and patience. Most of the gain comes from holding winners.

Connects to: Part 1 (corporate earnings), Part 3 (value/quality factors), the "small investor advantages" in Part 3B. Do after: pick one company you use daily (e.g. a listed retailer, bank or consumer brand). Classify it into one of the six categories and do the two-minute drill using its latest annual report.
